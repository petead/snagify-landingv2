There is a version of your check-out, twelve months from now, where the handover takes ten minutes, the deposit comes back in days, and nobody argues about a scratch. That version is built today, in the twenty minutes before your furniture arrives.

We have covered why the documentation is entirely your job in Dubai and where it fits in your first 48 hours. This is the how: the room-by-room checklist of what to capture, the spots everyone misses, and the two steps that turn a folder of photos into evidence.

## The method before the rooms

Three rules govern the whole exercise:

Empty apartment first. Before boxes, before movers. Furniture hides walls and floors, and every scratch discovered after moving day becomes ambiguous. The empty window is the cleanest baseline you will ever have.

Wide, then close. For each room: one or two wide shots that establish the space, then detail shots of anything notable, existing marks, worn spots, defects. A close-up of a scratch with no context photo proves little; the pair proves everything.

Everything on, everything open. Taps run, AC blows, oven heats, cupboards open. A photo of a closed appliance documents a box; a tested appliance documents a working one, or a defect you report today instead of paying for later.

## Room by room

**Every room, the base layer.** All four walls, the ceiling, the full floor. Doors: both faces, handles, locks working. Windows: glass, frames, mosquito screens, locks, and the seals (Dubai heat destroys seals, and failed seals become condensation disputes). Light switches and sockets: a quick shot of each wall's fittings, and test the lights.

**Living areas.** Balcony floor, railing and drainage hole (a blocked balcony drain plus one rainstorm equals a flooding argument). Curtain rails if fitted. Any built-in units, inside and out.

**Kitchen, the dispute factory.** Countertops from above and at an angle, existing chips and burns close up. Every appliance opened AND run: oven heated, hob rings lit, fridge and freezer cold with seals photographed, washing machine on a short cycle, dishwasher if present. Inside cupboards and drawers, hinges included. Under the sink: the pipework, dry. The sink itself, drainage flowing.

**Bathrooms.** Basin, WC (seat and flush), shower or tub. The grout and the silicone lines, close up: existing mold or gaps documented today are not your bill tomorrow. Water pressure and hot water, tested. Drainage speed, tested. The extractor fan, running. Under-basin pipework, dry.

**AC, the Dubai special.** Every unit blowing cold, photographed with its thermostat. Every remote, counted and working, they are a classic small deduction. Filters if accessible: a photo of their day-one state anchors the wear conversation a year from now. Any existing noise or weak zone: noted in writing today.

**The meters.** DEWA reading, photographed with the meter number visible. Chiller meter if the tower runs district cooling. Gas meter if applicable. These numbers are the other baseline of your tenancy: the financial one.

**Keys and access.** Count and photograph everything handed over: keys, access cards, parking remotes, mailbox key. Note the counts in the report. They get counted again at check-out, and the missing-remote charge is a small classic.

**Furnished units: the inventory layer.** Every item with its condition: sofas (cushions lifted, stains and wear noted), beds and mattresses, tables, curtains, every included appliance, kitchenware if listed. In furnished tenancies the deposit typically runs 10% of annual rent instead of 5%, so this layer is protecting double the money.

## The spots everyone misses

Ceilings (water-stain history lives there). Behind doors. Inside the oven. The washing machine's rubber seal. Balcony drainage. Window seals. The water heater switch and tank area. Under both sinks. Walk the list twice: the second pass catches what the first one assumed.

## The two steps that turn photos into evidence

A camera roll, even a thorough one, sits low on the evidence hierarchy: no structure, fragile dates, no acknowledgment from the other side. Two steps fix that.

Structure and date it. Organize the record room by room into a single document, each photo tied to its location, the whole thing time-stamped and linked to the tenancy. One structured report reads, translates and files as evidence; two hundred loose images do not.

Get it signed, or get it delivered. The landlord's or agent's signature at handover is the gold standard: the condition stops being your version and becomes an agreed fact. If they cannot or will not sign, send the complete report to landlord and agent by email the same day and keep the thread. A dated, delivered, unobjected-to record carries real weight, and a documented refusal to sign tends to work against the refuser, not you.

Then file it somewhere you will find in twelve months, alongside the meter photos and the key counts. Day one is done, and so is most of your check-out: when the tenancy ends, the same walkthrough against the same baseline turns the deposit conversation into a comparison instead of a negotiation.
