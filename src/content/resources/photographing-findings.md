# Photographing findings the right way

Two inspection reports can describe the same apartment and carry completely different weight. The difference is almost never the writing. It is the photos: whether a stranger, months later, can look at them and see exactly what was there, where, and how big. This tutorial teaches the technique in ten minutes, and it will make every report you produce harder to argue with. It also makes Snagify's AI work better for you, because the AI describes what your photos show, and better photos produce better drafts.

## Step 1: Always start wide (the context shot)

Every room begins with one or two wide photos from opposite corners, showing walls, floor, ceiling and windows together. The wide shot does two jobs: it proves the room was inspected as a whole, and it gives every detail photo an address. A close-up of a crack means little on its own; paired with a wide shot where you can locate that wall, it becomes evidence.

Hold the phone at chest height, keep it level, and get the ceiling line and floor line in frame when you can.

## Step 2: Then go close (the finding shot)

For every finding worth recording, take one dedicated close-up: near enough that the finding is unmistakable, far enough that a bit of context survives around it. Fill roughly a third of the frame with the finding itself.

One finding, one photo. Resist the urge to capture three findings in one frame: at check-out, each finding will be compared against its own baseline, and a photo that shows one thing clearly beats a photo that shows three things vaguely.

> [PHOTO 2: a close-up of a wall scratch, correctly framed]

## Step 3: Put the size in the frame

"A crack" is an opinion. "A crack approximately 80 cm along the ceiling joint" is a record. When a finding has a size, make the size visible: photograph along the full length of a crack, hold a key or a hand next to a small stain for scale, step back far enough that a large defect reads as large.

Snagify's AI picks up on visible scale and writes measured descriptions, and you can refine the estimate when you validate the draft. Measurements are the difference between a report that describes and a report that proves.

> [PHOTO 3: a crack photographed along its length, a hand for scale]

## Step 4: Fight the light, not with the flash

Dubai apartments swing between blinding daylight and dim corners, and both hide findings. Three habits fix most of it: shoot with the window behind you, not in front of you; move close enough that the finding is not lost in glare; and use the flash only for dark corners, never on glossy walls where it whites out the exact area you are documenting. If a finding only shows at an angle, shoot it at that angle, then add one straight-on photo for location.

> [PHOTO 4: the same wall stain shot against the light vs with the light, side by side]

## Step 5: Photograph the clean rooms too

The most skipped photo in any inspection is the room with nothing wrong. Skip it and the report has a hole: at check-out, a new finding in that room has no baseline to be compared against, and "it was already there" becomes an argument instead of a lookup.

A clean room needs its wide shots and nothing else. Thirty seconds now closes the door on the most common dispute there is.

## Step 6: Let the AI draft, then make it yours

After each photo, Snagify's AI suggests a description and finding tags. Your photos are its only eyes: a sharp, well-framed, well-lit photo produces a precise draft; a blurry one produces a vague draft you will have to rewrite. Review every suggestion, correct what is off, remove what you disagree with. Nothing enters the report without your validation.

Document honestly in both directions. Recording a real finding protects the tenant at check-out; recording a clean surface protects the landlord. The baseline serves whoever tells the truth, which is exactly why it wins disputes.

## Step 7: The 30-second self-check before you move on

Before leaving each room, scan your photos and ask three questions. Can a stranger locate every finding from the wide shot? Is every finding's size readable? Would I accept these photos as proof if I were the other party? If one answer is no, the photo costs ten seconds to retake now and an argument to live without later.

## The technique in one line

Wide for context, close for proof, size in frame, light behind you, clean rooms included, AI drafts validated. That is the whole craft, and it takes no longer than photographing carelessly.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many photos per room is enough?**
One or two wide shots plus one close-up per finding. A typical room lands between two and five photos. Coverage beats volume: fifty photos with no wide shots prove less than eight photos taken with this technique.

**Should I photograph inside cupboards and appliances?**
Open anything the tenant will use daily: wardrobes, kitchen cabinets, the oven, the washing machine drum. One photo each if they are clean, plus a close-up if there is a finding. Hidden areas are where "that was already broken" disputes are born.

**What if a finding doesn't show up on camera?**
Some findings resist photography: faint odours, a loose hinge, low water pressure. Photograph the area anyway for location, and record the finding in the notes when you validate the draft. The report carries both photos and validated text, and the note is part of the signed record.
